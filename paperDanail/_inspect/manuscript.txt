P000 (Normal): RESEARCH ON THE ROBUSTNESS OF DEEP LEARNING-BASED DC MOTOR CONDITION CLASSIFICATION USING ARMATURE CURRENT SIGNALS
P002 (Normal): Danail Slavov1[0009-0008-3151-4203], Marin Zhilevski1[0000-0002-4545-9718], Nikolay Yordanov1[0009-0009-1406-4754], Mikho Mikhov1[0000-0002-7768-5525], Veselin Krachunov1, Oleg Krol2[0000-0003-0193-2750], Vladimir Sokolov2[0000-0003-0459-1824]

P006 (Normal): Abstract - This study investigated deep learning-based classification of four operating conditions of a brushed permanent-magnet DC servo motor using armature-current-related voltage signals, with particular emphasis on generalisation across operating speeds and recording protocols. A selected set of 72 recordings covered nine commanded speed settings and two acquisition modes, with and without periodic rotation reversals. Signals were resampled to 20 kHz and segmented into overlapping 0.5 s windows. A conventional one-dimensional CNN and single- and two-channel InceptionTime configurations were assessed using recording-disjoint evaluation, an unseen-speed holdout, nine-fold leave-one-speed-out (LOSO) testing, and bidirectional cross-protocol transfer. The two-channel representation additionally incorporated a band-pass-filtered Hilbert envelope. Results were averaged over three random seeds and assessed at recording level. LOSO mean accuracies reached 98.61% and 98.15% for the one- and two-channel InceptionTime models, respectively. In contrast, cross-protocol mean accuracies were 75.93% and 74.07% when training without reversals, and 62.96% and 61.11% in the opposite direction. Direction-specific analysis did not identify reverse rotation or reversal transients as the sole explanation for reduced transfer performance. The envelope channel did not provide a consistent improvement. These findings distinguish robustness to unobserved speed settings from robustness to acquisition-protocol changes and underline the importance of representative training data.
P008 (Normal): Keywords:  DC motor; Condition monitoring; Armature current; Deep learning; InceptionTime; Generalisation; Rotation reversal.
P009 (Heading 1): 1. Introduction
P011 (Normal): Electrical current measurements offer an attractive basis for motor-condition monitoring when supplementary vibration or acoustic instrumentation is unavailable or undesirable. Classical motor-current signature analysis has demonstrated the diagnostic value of electrical signals, particularly for induction machines [1]. However, the characteristics of brushed DC servo motors and their converter-controlled operating regimes differ from those systems.
P012 (Normal): Artificial intelligence methods have been widely investigated for rotating-machinery fault diagnosis, encompassing both conventional machine learning and deep learning approaches [2]. Recent reviews highlight challenges associated with limited labelled data, changes in operating conditions and domain transfer [3, 4]. Deep learning architectures have also been extensively investigated for the classification of univariate and multivariate time series [5], with InceptionTime providing an effective convolutional architecture for time-series classification [6]. Nevertheless, high classification accuracy under familiar operating conditions does not necessarily imply reliable generalisation to conditions associated with a different data distribution, highlighting the importance of evaluation protocols that explicitly assess generalisation [4, 7]. Deep domain adaptation has also been investigated for rotating-machinery diagnosis under distribution shifts [8].
P013 (Normal): Related work by the research team examined time–frequency representations for rotating-machinery diagnosis [9] and vibro-acoustic manifestations of loosened motor foundations [10, 11]. The present investigation focused on an electrical signal related to armature current and assessed classification robustness using evaluation protocols with explicit separation of the original recordings.
P014 (Normal): The objectives were to compare a conventional CNN with InceptionTime, assess generalisation to unseen speed settings, quantify transfer between recording protocols with and without rotation reversals, and examine whether rotation direction or reversal transients explain cross-protocol errors. The contribution consists of a comparative evaluation of classification robustness under changes in operating speed and acquisition protocol.
P015 (Normal): The diagnostic task concerned the recognition of operating conditions rather than the estimation of instantaneous rotational speed. Because speed influences the electrical waveform, incidental associations between class labels and speed settings could affect classification performance. The experimental design therefore combined a conventional recording-disjoint split, leave-one-speed-out evaluation and bidirectional transfer between acquisition protocols.
P016 (Normal): The original recording was used as the unit of data separation. Consecutive windows from a single oscilloscope capture share operating conditions, acquisition settings and time-correlated fluctuations. Assigning such windows randomly to both training and testing can introduce information leakage and produce overly optimistic estimates of generalisation, as demonstrated in vibration-based bearing diagnosis [12]. Accordingly, all windows derived from a recording remained within its assigned partition.
P017 (Normal): The investigated electrical channel was used to assess whether the four defined operating conditions could be distinguished under changes in speed and reversal protocol. No direct comparison between current-based and vibration-based classification was performed.
P018 (Normal): The selected conditions included both mechanical and control-related modifications. A loosened foundation changes the mechanical boundary conditions of the motor, whereas non-optimal regulator gains change the behaviour of the closed-loop drive. Both modifications can affect the measured electrical waveform through different mechanisms. The study evaluated whether these conditions could be distinguished using a common time-series classification approach, without attempting to identify the underlying mechanisms directly.
P019 (Normal): Previous research has investigated the transferability of CNN features to unseen machine conditions [13], while broader reviews identify transfer between diagnostic tasks as an important issue for engineering applications [14]. These considerations motivate evaluation beyond familiar laboratory conditions. In the present study, testing on an acquisition protocol excluded from training provided a controlled assessment of sensitivity to changes in the operating sequence. This evaluation did not include adaptation using target-protocol data and should not be interpreted as validation on a different machine or industrial site.
P021 (Heading 1): 2. Materials and Methods
P022 (Heading 2): 2.1. Experimental recordings and classification task
P024 (Normal): To analyze the frequency content of the armature current, the power spectral density (PSD) is computed using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) for all motor conditions. The PSD estimates are obtained with the same preprocessing settings and are compared in three frequency ranges: 0 - 1,000 Hz, 0 - 10,000 Hz, and 0 - 100,000 Hz. The spectra for the four motor conditions at 50% speed are shown in Fig. 1.
P025 (Caption) [PIC]: Fig. 1. Power spectral density of the armature-current-related voltage for the four motor conditions at 50% speed: (a) 0 - 1,000 Hz, (b) 0 - 10,000 Hz, and (c) 0 - 100,000 Hz
P027 (Normal): The results in Fig. 1 indicate that the useful information is mainly concentrated below 1 kHz, where clear differences between the motor conditions are observed, especially in the low-frequency region and around the dominant spectral components. At higher frequencies, the spectra become increasingly similar and approach the noise floor. Therefore, a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter with a passband of 200 - 1,000 Hz is applied to suppress irrelevant frequency components, and the signal envelope is then computed using the Hilbert transform.
P028 (Normal): To further investigate the temporal characteristics of the measured signals, amplitude envelopes were extracted using a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter (200–1000 Hz), followed by the Hilbert transform. Figure 2 compares the resulting envelopes for the four investigated motor conditions over a representative one-second interval at 50% commanded speed, without rotation reversal. The envelopes exhibit condition-dependent differences, particularly in the amplitude variations associated with non-optimal regulator gains.
P030 (Normal) [PIC]: 
P031 (Caption): Fig. 2. Comparison of Hilbert-transform amplitude envelopes of armature-current-related voltage signals for four motor operating conditions at 50% commanded speed (10–11 s interval, without rotation reversal)
P033 (Normal): As illustrated in Fig. 2, the normal and loosened-foundation conditions exhibit relatively similar envelope characteristics, whereas the two regulator-related conditions show more pronounced amplitude variations within the selected interval. These observations motivated the investigation of an additional input representation incorporating the extracted envelope alongside the original current waveform. However, visual differences in individual signal segments do not necessarily translate into improved classification performance, and the effectiveness of the two-channel representation was therefore evaluated experimentally.
P034 (Normal): The experimental subset comprised 72 recordings from the multi-sensor dataset [6]: four labelled conditions, nine commanded speeds (1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50% and 60% of rated speed, corresponding to 20–1200 rpm), and two protocols. Only the nine common commanded speed settings up to 60% were used so that each class–protocol combination was represented at every evaluated speed (as the non-optimal speed-regulator condition was physically limited to 65% of rated speed). The investigated conditions were defined from the imposed experimental settings rather than post hoc clustering: normal operation (N), loosened motor foundation with all four mounting bolts loosened by hand (LF), non-optimal speed-regulator gain, and non-optimal current-regulator. Consequently, the classes describe known operating conditions of the laboratory apparatus rather than a general taxonomy of all possible DC motor failures. Each recording was approximately 20 s long and acquired at 1 MHz using a Rigol MSO5074 oscilloscope (channel 1, DC coupling, 1 V/div, −3 V offset). The motor operated without an external mechanical load. The dataset was acquired by the authors from a brushed permanent-magnet DC servo system [6].
P035 (Normal): Under the no-reversal protocol the motor rotated continuously in the forward direction. Under the with-reversal protocol approximately four manually initiated direction reversals occurred during each recording (approximately every 4 s). These events create short intervals of rapidly changing electrical behaviour, while the intervening intervals correspond to sustained forward or reverse rotation, meaning the same nominal speed setting produces different temporal sequences depending on the protocol. The acquisition channel was a voltage proportional to armature current; because its conversion coefficient was not established, amplitudes are reported in volts rather than calibrated amperes.
P037 (Heading 2): 2.2. Preprocessing and input representations
P039 (Normal): Signals were downsampled from 1 MHz to 20 kHz (a factor of 50) using polyphase resampling with anti-alias filtering to limit computational cost while retaining the lower-frequency signal content. Both network configurations received one-dimensional time-series samples rather than spectrograms, preserving temporal ordering. The one-channel representation comprised the resulting current-related voltage waveform. For the two-channel representation, a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter (200–1000 Hz) in second-order sections was applied in zero phase (forward–backward filtering), followed by the magnitude of the Hilbert analytic signal to obtain a non-negative amplitude envelope. Preliminary power-distribution analysis showed that a large fraction of the recorded signal energy was concentrated below 1 kHz, where thyristor converter ripple and commutation-related components contribute periodic structure. Because the envelope is derived deterministically from the same measured channel, the two-channel model does not represent multi-sensor fusion. All preprocessing parameters were kept identical across operating conditions and folds.
P040 (Normal): To reduce filter-boundary and Hilbert-transform edge effects and equalise both representations, 0.5 s was trimmed from each recording end. For a remaining duration T = 19 s, window length L = 0.5 s (10,000 samples at 20 kHz) and step S = 0.25 s (50% overlap), the resulting window count per recording is Nw = floor((T − L)/S) + 1 = 75 (5,400 windows across the 72 recordings). Splits were performed at recording level before window generation to prevent leakage between correlated windows. Input channels were standardised using the mean and standard deviation calculated exclusively from the training partition; the resulting statistics were applied unchanged to validation and test recordings.
P042 (Heading 2): 2.3. Models and training
P044 (Normal): The initial benchmark compared a conventional one-dimensional CNN with InceptionTime using the waveform alone (1-ch) or the aligned waveform and envelope (2-ch). The baseline CNN comprised three Conv1d–BatchNorm–ReLU–MaxPool blocks with 32, 64 and 128 output channels, kernel sizes 15, 9 and 5 (with padding to preserve temporal length), and pooling factor 4 in each block. Adaptive global average pooling and a linear layer mapped the 128 features to four class logits. InceptionTime used parallel multiscale convolutional pathways in a single network with four outputs and nf = 32, rather than an ensemble, isolating the effect of the input representation and architecture family. Models were trained on window-level cross-entropy loss using Adam (learning rate 0.001), batch size 32, and at most 100 epochs with an early stopping patience of 15 epochs. The checkpoint was selected using validation recording-level macro-F1, with validation loss as a tie-breaker. Three random seeds (42, 123, 456) were evaluated to expose sensitivity to initialisation and training stochasticity; no inferential significance test was claimed from the three-seed results.
P045 (Normal): If p(w, c) denotes the softmax probability for class c in window w, a recording-level prediction was obtained by averaging the softmax probability vectors of its Nw = 75 constituent windows and taking the maximum-probability class. Recording-level accuracy and macro-F1 were used as primary measures because overlapping windows are not independent observations.
P047 (Heading 2): 2.4. Evaluation protocols
P049 (Normal): The evaluation was organised to answer progressively more demanding generalisation questions. The initial benchmark used development speeds 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 30%, 40% and 60%, reserving 20% and 50% for unseen-speed testing. For each condition and protocol, five development-speed recordings were assigned to training, one to validation and one to standard testing, producing 40, 8, 8 and 16 recordings in the respective training, validation, standard-test and unseen-speed partitions. In nine-fold LOSO evaluation, each speed was held out in turn for testing (eight recordings); the next speed in a fixed cyclic schedule was used for validation (eight recordings), and the other seven speeds for training (56 recordings). Both protocols were represented in all three partitions, and the training and validation speed compositions consequently changed across folds. Cross-protocol experiments trained on recordings without reversal and tested on those with reversal (CR1), or reversed the roles (CR2). Each direction used 28 source-protocol training recordings (seven speeds), eight same-protocol validation recordings (two held-out speeds), and all 36 opposite-protocol test recordings (all nine speeds).

P084 (Caption): Figure 2: Aggregated LOSO confusion matrices over nine folds and three seeds. N: normal; LF: loosened foundation; SG: non-optimal speed-regulator gain; SG+CG: combined non-optimal speed- and current-regulator gains
P086 (Normal): The aggregated confusion matrices (Fig. 2, containing 54 evaluated predictions per true class across nine speeds, two protocols and three seeds) show a highly concentrated error distribution. All seven incorrect model–recording predictions (three for 1-ch and four for 2-ch) involved the non-optimal speed-regulator-gain class (SG) being misclassified as loosened foundation (LF), whereas normal operation (N) and the combined-regulator-gain condition (SG+CG) were classified with 100% accuracy throughout. The largest concentration of errors occurred at the 30% held-out speed, particularly for the forward-only SG recording, with single-seed errors also occurring at 20% (2-ch) and 40% (1-ch). Across all evaluations, these seven incorrect predictions corresponded to only three distinct physical recordings. Although six of the seven incorrect predictions concerned forward-only recordings, the sample of errors is too small to infer that the reversal protocol systematically improves speed generalisation.
P088 (Heading 2): 3.3. Cross-protocol evaluation
P090 (Normal): The cross-protocol experiments investigated transfer between recordings acquired with and without rotation reversal. Table 3 presents the recording-level results averaged across three seeds. In CR1, the one- and two-channel models achieved 75.93% and 74.07% accuracy; in CR2, the corresponding values were 62.96% and 61.11%. Transfer between protocols was therefore substantially more challenging than unseen-speed evaluation.
P092 (Normal): Table 3. Cross-protocol recording-level performance averaged over three seeds
P094 (Normal): Cross-protocol performance varied considerably between training seeds; in CR1, individual results ranged approximately from 56% to 97%, showing that while successful transfer was achievable for an individual checkpoint, the training procedure did not reliably produce protocol-invariant representations. Furthermore, the confusion structure shifted with the direction of transfer: in CR1, loosened-foundation (LF) recordings were frequently misclassified as non-optimal speed-regulator gain (SG), whereas in CR2, LF was often misclassified as normal operation (N), with some SG recordings also assigned to N. Although the two-channel model achieved a slightly higher macro-F1 in CR1 despite lower mean accuracy, both accuracy and macro-F1 were lower for the two-channel configuration in CR2, confirming no consistent benefit from the envelope channel.
P096 (Heading 2): 3.4. Direction-specific analysis
P098 (Normal): To test whether the CR1 performance reduction was caused solely by reverse rotation or reversal transients, the six previously trained CR1 checkpoints were evaluated without retraining on isolated forward- and reverse-rotation segments across the 34 annotated test recordings (Fig. 1). Windows overlapping each annotated reversal event were excluded using three alternative before/after margin settings: 0.10/0.20 s (narrow), 0.15/0.35 s (intermediate) and 0.50/0.50 s (conservative). For the intermediate margins, 150 windows were excluded from the two unannotated recordings, while the 34 annotated recordings contributed 1,186 forward, 820 reverse and 544 transition windows before recording-level aggregation. Table 4 reports the resulting mean recording-level accuracies.
P100 (Normal): Table 4. Direction-specific recording-level accuracy (%) for three transient-margin settings
P102 (Normal): Forward and reverse accuracies were nearly identical (74.51% vs. 73.53% for 1-ch) and remained relatively insensitive to the chosen transient-exclusion margins. One two-channel checkpoint achieved 97.06% recording-level accuracy in both directions, illustrating seed variability rather than a consistent representation advantage. Because removing transition windows and isolating forward-only segments within the with-reversal recordings did not restore accuracy to the LOSO level, the protocol-transfer degradation could not be explained solely by reverse rotation or immediate reversal transients.
P104 (Heading 1): 4. Discussion
P106 (Normal): The experiments demonstrated that armature-current-related signals contained discriminative information for the four investigated mechanical and control-related operating conditions without using vibration or acoustic measurements. This finding is consistent with the broader potential of electrical condition-monitoring signals, although prior induction-motor studies should not be treated as direct numerical comparators for the present converter-fed DC servo motor. An important interpretive caution concerns the meaning of the four labels: because the conditions were defined by specific laboratory interventions (bolt loosening and discrete controller gain changes), a classifier that separates them relies on stable electrical consequences of those interventions rather than directly identifying physical parameters.
P107 (Normal): A central methodological result was the distinction between speed generalisation and protocol generalisation. Both InceptionTime representations maintained mean LOSO accuracies above 98% across unseen commanded speeds when both acquisition protocols were present in training, whereas cross-protocol accuracy decreased markedly (61.11–75.93%) when the target operating procedure was absent from training. Thus, robustness must be defined with respect to a specific source of variation: strong interpolation across discrete speed setpoints does not imply equivalent robustness to a changed operating sequence, aligning with domain-shift challenges reported in the rotating-machinery diagnosis literature.
P108 (Normal): The direction-specific analysis showed no consistent deterioration during reverse rather than forward rotation, and removing reversal-adjacent transient windows had little effect on recording-level accuracy. Broader protocol-dependent factors—such as post-reversal settling dynamics, shorter steady-state intervals, or subtle operating-state differences—may therefore contribute to the transfer gap, although the present observational design cannot isolate a single causal mechanism. In addition, class separability depended on the training distribution: LOSO errors were concentrated exclusively in misclassifying SG as LF, whereas cross-protocol transfer introduced confusions between LF and SG in CR1 and between LF/SG and N in CR2.
P109 (Normal): Adding the 200–1000 Hz Hilbert-transform envelope increased preprocessing complexity and input dimensionality but did not consistently improve accuracy or macro-F1 over the single-channel waveform. The raw 20 kHz waveform evidently retains the primary discriminative information accessible to the multiscale convolutional filters on this dataset, making the simpler single-channel input a reasonable default, though other filter bands, envelope methods or motor systems may behave differently.
P110 (Normal): The study was limited to one no-load laboratory motor and converter arrangement, short recordings, three training seeds, a benchmark-only comparison with the baseline CNN, and eight independent recordings per held-out speed. While overlapping windows increased the training sample count to 5,400, they did not increase the number of independent physical acquisitions, which is why strict recording-level partitioning and probability aggregation were enforced throughout. For future work, a full factorial acquisition protocol with repeated independent captures of continuous-forward, continuous-reverse and reversal-containing sequences across multiple external loads would help separate direction effects, transient effects and run-to-run variability, while domain-generalisation or protocol-invariant training methods should be evaluated with target-protocol recordings strictly held out.
P112 (Heading 1): 5. Conclusions
P114 (Normal): This study investigated the robustness of deep learning-based DC motor condition classification using armature-current-related voltage signals under variations in operating speed and rotation-reversal protocol. A conventional CNN and two InceptionTime representations were assessed across four operating conditions.
P115 (Normal): The models demonstrated strong generalisation to previously unseen speed settings. Nine-fold LOSO mean recording-level accuracies reached 98.61% and 98.15% for one- and two-channel InceptionTime, respectively. In contrast, transfer between acquisition protocols resulted in lower and more variable accuracy. Forward/reverse analysis did not attribute this reduction exclusively to rotation direction or reversal transients. Adding a Hilbert-transform envelope did not consistently improve classification.
P116 (Normal): The findings distinguish speed robustness from protocol robustness and emphasise representative training data and evaluation procedures. Future work will investigate more diverse recordings, protocol-invariant training and validation across additional motor systems.
